V

Valentine Roux

CNRS / Université Paris Nanterre (UMR 8068 TEMPS)

Chaîne opératoire analysis; ethnoarchaeology of ceramic production; social transmission of technical traditions

Director of Research Emerita at the CNRS; long affiliated with UMR 8068 CrÉATiS / TEMPS (Université Paris Nanterre / CNRS). Leader of the French school of chaîne opératoire analysis: through ethnoarchaeology of pottery (India, Africa, Levant), experiments on motor schemes and agent-based modelling, she demonstrates that technical choices are social choices — technological traditions persist, spread or fossilise along the boundaries of social networks (~7,200 citations). Co-editor, with T. Ingold, of Tools, Language and Cognition in Human Evolution (CUP 1993). Key works: Ceramics and Society (Springer 2019); 'Persisting technological boundaries' (Journal of Anthropological Archaeology 48, 2017); 'Chaîne opératoire, technological networks and sociological interpretations' (CPAU-UG 30, 2020). Her framework decodes assemblages of the Grand Canal's kiln and craft sites as coalitions of technical choices revealing who learned from whom, along which routes.

https://umrtemps.cnrs.fr/en/membre/roux-valentine-2/

Taste

A taste for the granularity of making — paste preparation, coiling, firing, the kinematics of the gesture — refused to be flattened into typological classes; style alone is never explanation.

Quality

Unusual methodological range: long-term ethnoarchaeological fieldwork, motor-cognition experiments and agent-based simulation held together by one question — how technical traditions are transmitted.

Spirit

Objects are social facts: every operational chain encodes a 'coalition of technical choices'; if a difference can be made to matter socially, it can be made to matter analytically — decode, then model.

Character

Direct, polemical and constructive; more interested in designing the decisive test than in winning the school debate; a trainer of a whole French school of technological analysis.

Concerns

Persistence and diffusion of technological traditions; social boundaries and networks in the transmission of innovations; learning, bodily techniques and standardisation; what ceramics tell us about who learned from whom.

Philosophy

The chaîne opératoire as a matrix of the social: technical sequences are crystallised social relations; transmission is never diffusion but learning along network boundaries.
