Literature Review

The review as groundwork

This page is the project's reading groundwork: a verified, annotated review of the scholars whose work gives the project its premises about movement, place, material life, heritage and regeneration. It is designed to be a living reference — the basis for ongoing brainstorming, the combing of ideas and viewpoints, and the transition into substantive research. The companion document (materials/LITERATURE_REVIEW.md) carries the full annotated bibliography, keyed to the mapping of each source to the research questions.

A synthesis in six dimensions

The review organises every involved scholar through six dimensions. Premises: things act; walking makes knowledge; heritage works for future generations; change is the baseline. Perspectives: four analytical lenses — walking/movement, materiality/things/ruins, heritage futures, and connectivity/corridors. Ethos: embrace change, befriend decay, respect local livelihoods, answer to future generations. Method: the three-walk method, following one thing, food-tracing, island comparison, digital ethnography, narrative policy analysis. Practice: field cycles in Beijing, Gotland and Southern Öland, with regeneration observation. Persona: each scholar's academic taste, quality, spirit, character, concerns and philosophy — read from their verified works and positions.

How to read the portraits

The portraits below are academic persona sketches [project reading], derived from each scholar's verified works and institutional positions — never from private life or speculation. Each portrait carries six dimensions: taste (what they are drawn to), quality (scholarly strengths), spirit (working stance), character (temperament), concerns (what keeps them up at night), and philosophy (their deeper commitments). Selected works are prioritised by relevance to this project and source-labelled [A] primary / [B] authoritative secondary.

Extended circle

Around the five core scholars sits an extended circle of verified collaborators and co-authors: Þóra Pétursdóttir (Ruin Memories; Theory Adrift with Olsen), Caitlin DeSilvey (Curated Decay; palliative curation), Sharon Macdonald (Memorylands; Heritage Futures), Rodney Harrison (Heritage: Critical Approaches; Heritage Futures editor), Anders Högberg (Cultural Heritage and the Future with Holtorf; 'No future in archaeological heritage management?'), Jo Vergunst (Ways of Walking with Ingold), Gustav Wollentz and Samantha May (co-authors on heritage-futures), the Shanks–Webmoor–Witmore triangle (Archaeology: The Discipline of Things), the Gotland–Rapa Nui team of Poort, Persson-Fischier, Elf Donaldson and Schaub, and Gillette's co-authors Emma Boyd, Elizabeth Bennett and Milena Arias Schreiber.

Verification & open items

Every citation on this page has been web-verified, and sources are labelled [A] primary / [B] authoritative secondary / [C] general / [D] unverified clue. Maris Boyd Gillette

University of Gothenburg

Anthropology; China; material culture; food; labour

University of Gothenburg

Full profile →

Professor of Anthropology, School of Global Studies, University of Gothenburg (since 2017). Previously at Haverford College, Pennsylvania. Contributes China, material culture, economic life, labour, sustainability, food and material production, and community and museum engagement. Key works: Between Mecca and Beijing (2000, Stanford UP); China's Porcelain Capital (2016, Bloomsbury); work on mining heritage tourism in China (Journal of Heritage Tourism, 2024, with E. Boyd); on Swedish herring fisheries (Sociologia Ruralis, 2026). Evidence: University of Gothenburg staff records [A].

https://www.gu.se/en/about/find-staff/marisgillette

Taste

A taste for the concrete and the everyday as a window: a bowl, a herring, a coal mine — small tangible things carrying large historical transformations.

Quality

Deep, longitudinal ethnography; bilingual archival and field scholarship across China and Sweden; a willingness to start over in a new field and bring the old skills along.

Spirit

Intellectual humility before material production: take seriously what people make, buy, copy and eat — these are not trivia but the site where capitalism and culture meet.

Character

Quiet, meticulous, patient and unflashy; an ethnographer who lets sources and craft speak rather than performing authority.

Concerns

Labour, fabrication and copying; urban consumption and modernization in China; mining-heritage tourism; Swedish small-scale fisheries and food sustainability.

Philosophy

Materialist anthropology of livelihood: economic life is the strongest tracer of cultural relations; sustainability is a relational question of life, not a label.

Cornelius Holtorf

Linnaeus University, Kalmar

Heritage futures; regeneration; future generations

Linnaeus University; photo Johannes Rydström

Full profile →

Professor of Archaeology, Department of Cultural Sciences, Linnaeus University, Kalmar (since 2008); UNESCO Chair on Heritage Futures (since 2017, renewed 2021). Contributes heritage futures, future generations, uncertainty, regeneration, transformation, and stakeholder engagement. Key works: Heritage Futures (Harrison, DeSilvey, Holtorf et al. 2020, UCL Press); Cultural Heritage and the Future (2021, Routledge); 'Embracing Change' (World Archaeology 50(4), 2018); 'An Archaeology of Growth and Regeneration' (Kalmar Studies in Archaeology XIV, 2022, pp. 85-123). Evidence: Linnaeus University staff page [A].

https://corneliusholtorf.com

Taste

A taste for the counterintuitive question: what will heritage do for people a decade — or a century — from now? The future tense as the most interesting tense in heritage studies.

Quality

A communicator and convenor: UNESCO Chair, multi-partner volumes, plain prose; turns avant-garde frameworks (heritage futures, foresight literacy) into usable, testable practice.

Spirit

Embrace change — heritage is about building capacity for transformation, not freezing the past; optimistic, pragmatic and radically forward-looking.

Character

Open-handed, gregarious and scenario-minded; thrives on dialogue with stakeholders, artists, students and publics rather than monologue.

Concerns

Future generations and uncertainty; heritage standards and financing; nuclear waste as future heritage; regeneration at Gamla Skogsby; climate change and heritage practice.

Philosophy

Heritage-futures: heritage is future-making, not past-restoration; pasts are resources for building viable futures, and uncertainty is a source, not a problem.

Tim Ingold

University of Aberdeen

Social anthropology; wayfaring; human-environment relations

Photo: Caroline Dear, via timingold.com

Full profile →

Emeritus Professor of Social Anthropology, University of Aberdeen (Fellow of the British Academy; CBE 2022). Retired 2018 but remains highly active with publications through 2026 (~109,000 citations). Provides an epistemology of walking, wayfaring, attention, practical knowledge, and human-environment relations. Key works: The Perception of the Environment (2000); Lines: A Brief History (2007); Being Alive (2011); The Life of Lines (2015); Imagining for Real (2022). The field should be a place of knowledge production, not merely data collection.

https://www.timingold.com

Taste

A taste for unobserved everyday things — lines, walking, string, basketweaving — treated as epistemological protagonists rather than examples. Revulsion against reducing the world to objects-of-representation.

Quality

Rare clarity at great depth; wide erudition spanning anthropology, ecology, art and education; concepts (wayfaring, meshwork, attention, correspondence) that travel far beyond one discipline.

Spirit

The field is a place of knowledge production, not data collection; 'not knowing and paying attention' as a method and a virtue; writing as attention to the world's self-revelation.

Character

Gentle, relentless and quietly radical; a teacher-writer who changes his mind in print and asks his readers to keep their lines open and unfinished.

Concerns

Human-environment correspondence, dwelling and livelihood; how attention and practical skills shape knowledge; the education of the next generation; the future of knowledge itself.

Philosophy

Process-relational ontology: things are not objects but ongoing movement-lines; 'being-in-becoming'; life as a meshwork of interwoven, never-finished kinds of growth.

Helene Martinsson-Wallin

Uppsala University, Campus Gotland

Archaeology; landscape; historical ecology; sustainability

Uppsala University

Full profile →

Professor of Archaeology, Uppsala University, Campus Gotland (30+ years on Gotland). Contributes deep time, archaeology, landscape, historical ecology, sustainability, fisheries, tourism, and long-term human-environment relations. Key works include Martinsson-Wallin 1994 (PhD on Easter Island ahu) and Poort et al. 2021 (Sustainability 13(11): 6302) on Gotland-Rapa Nui comparative cultural tourism. Evidence: Uppsala University staff records [A].

https://www.uu.se/en/contact-and-organisation/staff?query=N12-2422

Taste

Islands as living laboratories — comparing Gotland with Rapa Nui and the Baltic littoral; a taste for deep time that meets the present in sustainability questions.

Quality

Steadfast longitudinal grounding (decades on Gotland), methodical integration of archaeology, historical ecology, tourism and fisheries; evidence-led comparative practice.

Spirit

Respect for deep time and for the people who live with it now; patience — the willingness to let one place teach you for decades.

Character

Calm, local-first, collaborative and pragmatic; a bridge-builder between Swedish heritage management, island communities and South Pacific scholarship.

Concerns

Island sustainability; tourism authenticity and belonging; coastal communities and fisheries; long-term human-environment feedback; living heritage on Gotland.

Philosophy

Historical ecology framing: present problems read through centuries of human–environment coupling; each island carries its own logic — comparison respects difference.

Bjørnar Olsen

UiT The Arctic University of Norway

Archaeology; materiality; contemporary archaeology; ruins

UiT The Arctic University of Norway

Full profile →

Professor of Archaeology, UiT The Arctic University of Norway (since 1994). Contributes materiality, things, contemporary archaeology, ruins, and infrastructures. Key works: In Defense of Things (2010); Archaeology: The Discipline of Things (2012); Ruin Memories (2014); Unruly Heritage (2025). His involvement in the Arctic Silk Road project at UiT examines contemporary infrastructure, anticipated connectivity and local uncertainty across northern regions.

https://uit.no/ansatte/bjornar.olsen

Taste

A taste for the leftovers of modernity — ruins, decommissioned infrastructures, off-season fishing camps — rather than canonical monuments. Contemporary things as the honest face of our own time.

Quality

A theorist who grounds ontology in fieldworn material; co-author of one of the most cited manifestos of symmetrical archaeology; able to write both sharply and simply.

Spirit

Anti-humanist courage — things are not passive background but co-agents of social life; a plea to stop reading everything through the human.

Character

Unsentimental, unpretentious, uncompromising; a steady voice that faces decay, abandonment and the unruly side of heritage without flinching.

Concerns

Modern ruins and material memory; 'unruly heritage' in the Anthropocene; Arctic infrastructures and anticipated connectivity; how things outlast the people who made them.

Philosophy

New materialism / symmetrical ontology: things 'push back', have memory-like endurance, and hold agency in their own right; the past is not only in the mind.

Valentine Roux

CNRS / Université Paris Nanterre (UMR 8068 TEMPS)

Chaîne opératoire analysis; ethnoarchaeology of ceramic production; social transmission of technical traditions

Full profile →

Director of Research Emerita at the CNRS; long affiliated with UMR 8068 CrÉATiS / TEMPS (Université Paris Nanterre / CNRS). Leader of the French school of chaîne opératoire analysis: through ethnoarchaeology of pottery (India, Africa, Levant), experiments on motor schemes and agent-based modelling, she demonstrates that technical choices are social choices — technological traditions persist, spread or fossilise along the boundaries of social networks (~7,200 citations). Co-editor, with T. Ingold, of Tools, Language and Cognition in Human Evolution (CUP 1993). Key works: Ceramics and Society (Springer 2019); 'Persisting technological boundaries' (Journal of Anthropological Archaeology 48, 2017); 'Chaîne opératoire, technological networks and sociological interpretations' (CPAU-UG 30, 2020). Her framework decodes assemblages of the Grand Canal's kiln and craft sites as coalitions of technical choices revealing who learned from whom, along which routes.

https://umrtemps.cnrs.fr/en/membre/roux-valentine-2/

Taste

A taste for the granularity of making — paste preparation, coiling, firing, the kinematics of the gesture — refused to be flattened into typological classes; style alone is never explanation.

Quality

Unusual methodological range: long-term ethnoarchaeological fieldwork, motor-cognition experiments and agent-based simulation held together by one question — how technical traditions are transmitted.

Spirit

Objects are social facts: every operational chain encodes a 'coalition of technical choices'; if a difference can be made to matter socially, it can be made to matter analytically — decode, then model.

Character

Direct, polemical and constructive; more interested in designing the decisive test than in winning the school debate; a trainer of a whole French school of technological analysis.

Concerns

Persistence and diffusion of technological traditions; social boundaries and networks in the transmission of innovations; learning, bodily techniques and standardisation; what ceramics tell us about who learned from whom.

Philosophy

The chaîne opératoire as a matrix of the social: technical sequences are crystallised social relations; transmission is never diffusion but learning along network boundaries.
