Methodology

Let the field change the researcher

Before entering a field site, record: What do I expect? During fieldwork: What do I actually observe? Afterward: What was wrong or incomplete in my original understanding? The field notebook thus contains three explicit categories — EXPECTED, OBSERVED, REVISED — and one more: SURPRISE.

The three-walk method

Walk A — the researcher's walk, alone. Walk B — the local walk, with a resident or long-term participant asking 'What matters here?'. Walk C — the practitioner walk, with someone working in heritage, tourism, food, archaeology, museums or local government asking 'What do outsiders misunderstand?'. The differences among these walks become data.

Following one thing

At each field site, choose one tangible thing and follow it — grain, fish, vegetables, ceramics, brick, salt, textiles, a craft, a food. Do not begin by asking what the object means. First ask: Where does it come from? Where does it go? Who touches it? Where does its mode of transport change? Who gives it meaning?

Following food

Food is one of the strongest cross-scale tracers. A food item can connect: soil/water → production → farmer → road → canal → market → city → household/restaurant → memory/identity → tourism → future sustainability. This links naturally to regenerative heritage work on Öland, where archaeological soil, plants and food explore how the past can generate new life.

Scope discipline

The Grand Canal–Silk Roads relationship: a spotting lens, not a separate giant historical programme. Food: a tracer of relationships, not an independent discipline. Tourism: a revealing contemporary context, not the main subject. AI: a research instrument, not the project's identity.
