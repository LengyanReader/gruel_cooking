### EVENT 1: THE STRAIT CLOSES — AND THE WORLD ECONOMY FEELS IT

The ongoing military conflict with Iran has closed the Strait of Hormuz to most of its usual traffic, including tankers that carry about 20 percent of the world's oil from the Persian Gulf to the Arabian Sea. The Strait of Hormuz is approximately 21 miles wide at its narrowest point. Through that bottleneck flows roughly one-fifth of the world's oil supply. Its closure — even partial, even contested, even temporary — is not merely a military event. It is a civilizational disruption of the first order. From waiving the Jones Act to rerouting oil through the Red Sea, governments are doing their best to make up for the crisis, but the workarounds are partial, expensive, and slow.

British energy major Shell said production at the world's largest gas-to-liquids plant, Pearl GTL, has stopped after Iranian missiles struck the Ras Laffan complex in Qatar. A Kuwaiti oil refinery came under Iranian drone attack early Friday. Sirens in Israel warned of incoming fire while explosions boomed over Tehran from Israeli strikes as the country marked the Persian New Year.

**My Comment:** The Strait of Hormuz closure is the economic equivalent of a civilizational artery being severed. Every economic projection made before February 28 — for inflation, for growth, for energy transition, for the cost of everything from plastic to pharmaceuticals to food — must now be rewritten. What makes this culturally as well as economically significant is the revelation of how fragile the architecture of globalization actually is. We built a world that runs on a chokepoint 21 miles wide in the Persian Gulf, and now the chokepoint is contested. The logistics of abundance — the miracle by which virtually anything can be delivered to virtually anywhere for remarkably little cost — turns out to have a throat. And someone has their hands on it.

### EVENT 5: NOWRUZ — THE 3,000-YEAR-OLD FESTIVAL MEETS THE 21ST DAY OF WAR

Today — this very day — is Nowruz. Nowruz has been celebrated for more than 3,000 years, with some saying the festival may be up to 4,500 years old. For Iranians, Nowruz is a celebration of life, rebirth, and the triumph of light over darkness as winter gives way to spring.

"Time feels suspended in some ways," said Nazanin, a 36-year-old Tehran resident. "We are on the precipice of something, on the edge every day but we never fall over. The usual markers of time like Chaharshanbe Suri and Nowruz have come along and mean so little."

**My Comment:** Netanyahu's weaponization of Nowruz — claiming that bombing Tehran's squares is a gift to Iranians' ability to celebrate their New Year — is one of the most extraordinary examples of cultural instrumentalization in modern political rhetoric. It inverts the actual reality — in which security forces are *banning* Nowruz gatherings and firing on celebrants — and reframes destruction as liberation. This is not merely cynical. It is a specific technique of information warfare: the appropriation of your enemy's cultural symbols to narrate your own violence as their liberation. It will be studied in communications departments for years. More importantly: Iranians themselves, many of whom have genuinely complex feelings about their government, appear largely to see through it. The Iranian who told NPR "I do not know how some talk about protests again — from my own alley, anyone who wants to join a protest, they will not make it even outside this alley" is not the grateful liberated person the narrative requires.

The deeper cultural truth of Nowruz 2026 is this: for millennia, Nowruz has carried a simple message: despite any turmoil we face now, there will be a future. A civilization that has survived Mongol invasion, Ottoman siege, British colonial manipulation, American-backed coup, revolutionary upheaval, eight years of war with Iraq, and four decades of theocratic rule is not going to be culturally extinguished by three weeks of airstrikes. The haft-sin tables are set. The hyacinths are in the market. The fish are in the bowl. The new year has arrived. And 3,000 years of cultural continuity is making a quiet, powerful argument that no military campaign can fully answer.

**My Comment:** What the eight-week retrospective reveals, with painful clarity, is the absence of what strategists call "circuit breakers" — the institutional mechanisms, diplomatic channels, back-channel communications, and red-line agreements that in previous eras might have interrupted the accumulation before it reached the point of no return. The arms control architecture that provided circuit breakers during the Cold War — SALT, START, NPT, the Vienna channels — had been systematically eroded over the preceding decade. When you remove the circuit breakers, the current flows uninterrupted until something breaks. Eight weeks ago, the conditions for catastrophe were fully assembled. The catastrophe was not yet inevitable — but it was available. And it was chosen.
