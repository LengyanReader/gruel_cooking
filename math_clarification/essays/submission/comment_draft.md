## Synopsis

### Mathematical truth is not a matter of consensus

Claims that machines have begun to do original mathematics are no longer speculative. They are also frequently misread. Recent results show that AI systems can solve olympiad problems at medal standard, improve objective computational targets, contribute to the disproof of a conjecture open since 1946, and produce formal derivations that a proof kernel accepts. None of this settles what mathematics is, and none of it can be settled by counting how many people approve.

The public debate merges three questions that should be separated. The first is truth: does a proposition hold? The second is validity: does a derivation follow from stated premises? The third is admissibility: is this the problem the field posed, is the statement expressed in the intended sense, and does the result enter the literature? A machine can be decisive about the second and, in principle, about parts of the first. The third has no mechanical form. Certification accordingly takes place in machine-checked, hybrid and community settings, each with different failure modes and remedies.

The 2024–2026 record shows why the distinction matters. The public disputes that attracted attention were, in that record, about framing, disclosure, attribution and admissibility rather than about the validity of a derivation. A benchmark score detached from its evaluation conditions set off a public row; an Olympiad result became a controversy through the timing of its release; a Millennium Prize announcement became contested over which statement had been proved. Formal verification sharpens these differences rather than dissolving them: a kernel can confirm that a derivation is valid for a specified statement, while the choice of that statement remains an act of mathematics.

A second difficulty is the verification bottleneck. Generating a candidate result and reconstructing its argument are different forms of work, and most of the durable cost falls in the stages after generation. Formal proofs can change what people understand rather than merely confirm what they already knew, but a verified artifact that nobody can reconstruct supplies a fact without the understanding that lets the fact be used, extended or taught.

The institutional response should follow the separation of the questions. Results should be disclosed symmetrically, including failures; certifiers should be designated before results are known; admissibility should be registered separately from derivation; and verification should be funded as skilled labour. This arrangement is neither prohibition nor surrender. It is a division of labour, and the arrangement can be tested: it would be refuted by a documented false machine-checked result, or by evidence that these settings do not predict where disputes arise.

The Review audits that record event by event, maps the voices raised around it, and uses the audit to revisit the older questions the record has forced open: what mathematics is, who mathematicians are, and how the work of mathematics should be divided between people and machines.
