# 数学的危机，还是数学家的危机？——AI 时代的核验、语言与职业分工 / A Crisis of Mathematics or of the Mathematicians? Verification, the Language Thesis, and the Division of Labor in Mathematics in the Age of AI

## Abstract

For thirty years the advance of artificial intelligence in mathematics has been narrated as a crisis of mathematics itself. This essay argues that the narration mistakes its object. Reading the recent results against three earlier technical streams (1954–1965, 1968–1997, 2000–2020) and against the philosophical and sociological traditions that posed the same question decades ago, it shows that the positions taken in 2026 re-run an old argument with the burden of proof shifted from capability to authority. Taking the results publicly recorded between 2024 and 2026 as its case material, the essay sorts them on five questions — honest framing, reproducibility, independent certification, symmetric disclosure, and whether an answer was found or made — into a "staircase" running from machine-graded to community-adjudicated layers, a preliminary census of eleven such events being reported in an appendix. A language thesis (signifier and signified), read together with the Gödel–Turing limit results, yields three claims: exactness is a property of a sign-system rather than of what it denotes; verification certifies grammar, never the object; and the "crisis" shows no symptom in mathematical content — what is under renegotiation is employment, authority, prestige, and the meaning of professional labor. On this basis "mathematician" is reconstructed as a four-rung ladder of labor, the genius cult is decomposed into five social causes, and a layered division of labor between machines and humans is proposed and then corrected by cross-civilizational evidence. The criterion offered for "groundbreaking" work is that a breakthrough moves the border rather than the frontier: machines have broken walls inside given languages, with no public record yet of language-building — a standard that, on the evidence of Ramanujan's unposed questions, few humans satisfy either. The crisis, correctly named, is a social crisis of the mathematicians; its normative consequence is an agenda of disclosure, certification, and verification institutions rather than prohibition.

**Keywords:** artificial intelligence and mathematics; automated theorem proving; proof assistant; formal verification; certification regime; signifier and signified; mathematical community; verification crisis; Leiden Declaration

## 1. The Question, Restated

For thirty years the progress of AI has been narrated as a siege: Deep Blue defeated Kasparov in 1997; AlphaGo reached the summit of Go in 2016; from 2024 machines entered olympiad mathematics; in May 2026 a conjecture open since 1946 was disproved by a model [1]; and in September of the same year a Millennium Prize problem was declared "apparently" settled, with its authorship contested in public [2][3]. Each stage was announced as the fall of the last stronghold of human mind; none of the strongholds held. This essay does not continue that narrative but replaces its question. Whether mathematics is in crisis is, on the evidence assembled below, indeterminate; whether mathematicians are in crisis is the empirically tractable question — and the two are systematically conflated in public discourse.

The argument moves through five turns. It begins by reading the present controversy against its own prehistory — three technical streams (1954–1965, 1968–1997, 2000–2020), the philosophical and sociological traditions that posed the same question earlier, and the fast literature of 2021–2026 — and by naming what these bodies of work leave unconnected (Section 2). It then fixes the vocabulary the rest of the essay depends on and rebuilds the syllogism on which the controversy actually rests (Section 3). The case material — the period's loudest results, read through five questions: was the claim honestly framed; is it reproducible; was it independently certified; were failures disclosed as freely as successes; and did the machine find an answer already present in the literature or construct one that did not exist — is sorted into a staircase of certification regimes (Section 4). The same evidence is then read through the signifier/signified distinction and the incompleteness results, to derive a bound on what verification can certify (Section 6). The controversy itself is treated as data: five published positions are shown to share one syllogism whose minor premise ("mathematics is a game") is true only of the formalized fragment (Section 5); the profession is modeled as a ladder of labor rather than a type of person (Section 7); a demarcation question — can machines pose groundbreaking problems — is answered with an explicit criterion (Sections 8–9). Section 10 gathers the conclusions. An appendix, set after them, reports a preliminary census of the eleven events: the corpus rules and their exclusions, the ordered decision rule by which a certification regime is assigned, the coding behind the five questions, the material and method limits within which the reading is offered, and the successor design that would turn this ordering into a test.

Three things should be said about the evidence before the argument begins. First, several of the 2025–2026 episodes rest on institutional announcements and secondary reportage; all such items are tiered and flagged in the reference apparatus (一手 / 权威版本 / 学界共识·解读 / 存疑), and contested figures are cited as reported, not as settled. Second, what follows is an analysis of the mathematics community's verification practices, not of any formal system's soundness: no mathematical result of its own is claimed. Third, the coding reported in the appendix was executed by a single coder on public material alone; no inter-coder statistic is offered, which is why the cards, the decision rule and the cell-level citations are published in full — so that the coding may be disputed item by item rather than taken on trust.

## 2. Literature Review: The Evolution of Prior Work

The 2026 controversy is rarely read against its own prehistory, which is one reason it keeps being described as unprecedented. This section reconstructs the prior work along three technical streams (1954–1965, 1968–1997, 2000–2020), two argumentative traditions (philosophical and sociological), and one fast-moving body of recent literature (2021–2026), and closes with the gaps the present study addresses. Inclusion was governed by three tests: a work had either (i) changed what machines were expected to do in mathematics, (ii) supplied a criterion by which a mathematical result counts as certified, or (iii) formulated a position still live in the 2026 debate. The corpus is deliberately bounded rather than exhaustive: it is the cited-and-citable literature that the 2026 participants themselves invoke, together with the works that introduced each of the paper's five analytic categories (certification regime, kernel-checked proof, signifier/signified, ladder of labor, border/frontier). Every item is tiered in the reference list according to the reliability rules declared in Section 1; where a claim about an older episode rests on secondary reconstruction rather than on the primary publication, the entry says so.

### 2.1 Stream I (1954–1965): the founding act — deduction as machine work

The mechanization of deduction begins with Hao Wang's decision procedures for substantial fragments of the predicate calculus, reported in 1954 and popularized in his 1960 lectures, where a machine was shown to produce proofs of theorems from *Principia Mathematica* without human intermediate steps [4][5]. In the same year, Newell, Shaw and Simon completed the Logic Theorist, a heuristic search program that reproduced and in one case improved proofs in the propositional calculus of *Principia* [6]. Both projects already carried the modern ambiguity: Wang's was a proof *generator* whose outputs were checkable by inspection of the derivation, while the Logic Theorist advertised itself as evidence about human thinking. The decisive technical turn was Robinson's resolution principle, which replaced a forest of search heuristics with a single inference rule complete for refutation, and thereby made automated theorem proving a field with a shared engine rather than a collection of curiosities [7]. Two features of this founding period persist through the 2026 disputes: the machine's competence was defined *inside* a formal system, and the question of what the system *means* was never on the machine's side of the ledger.

### 2.2 Stream II (1968–1997): proof checkers, and the first demarcation scandal

de Bruijn's AUTOMATH made the second split explicit: a proof is an object, and the reliability of a machine-checked proof may rest on a tiny kernel whose correctness is all that need be trusted [8] — the design principle that the Lean family still follows, and whose limits Section 6 examines. The scandal arrived with the four-color theorem: Appel and Haken's 1976–77 reduction to a machine-enumerated case analysis forced a human community to decide, in public and without a formal checker, whether something was a proof [9]. Tymoczko drew the philosophical conclusion — that mathematics had become a priori but fallible, its certitude resting on communal procedures of checking rather than on incorrigible deduction [10] — and DeMillo, Lipton and Perlis argued the symmetric thesis for software: formal verification would not displace the social processes that, in both mathematics and computing, are what actually confer acceptance [11]. Lakatos, published in the same year as Appel and Haken, supplied the ancestor of all such views: mathematics progresses through proofs and refutations, argument and counterexample, and its certainty is a product of that dialectic rather than of deduction alone [12]. When McCune's Otter produced a proof of the Robbins conjecture in 1997 — a conjecture that had stood since the 1930s, resolved by a machine in a form no human had written — the pattern was already legible: an unquestionable result whose intelligibility had to be reconstructed afterward by readers [13]. Every element of the 2026 dispute over what counts as proved is present, in nuce, in 1979.

### 2.3 Stream III (2000–2020): proof assistants enter the research mainstream

Gowers' distinction between mathematicians who build theory and mathematicians who solve problems gave the community a vocabulary in which machine substitution could be debated by stratum rather than in bulk [14]. Avigad supplied the epistemological counterpart: formal proof does not merely confirm a theorem but changes what counting as understanding and as method amounts to, without exhausting either [15][16]. The empirical test case was Hales' Kepler conjecture: after a submission to a refereed journal ended in a partial endorsement because no referee team could complete the check, the proof was re-verified in HOL Light and Isabelle and published as a formally verified result — the first demonstration that community-scale formal verification was possible, and at what cost [17]. The Lean library mathlib then converted the tool from an artifact of foundational curiosity into working infrastructure, a campaign whose current form is documented in Section 5 (Buzzard's program). What this stream established, and what the 2026 record presupposes, is that the *certification layer* had migrated into software while the *significance layer* had not moved at all.

### 2.4 The philosophical counterpart: the old question "can a machine do mathematics?"

The capability question is older than the community that now poses it. Turing proposed replacing it with a behavioral test in 1950 [18]. Pólya had already made invention itself a teachable algebra of heuristics [19], and Hadamard, from mathematicians' testimony, located mathematical work in a combinatorics of elements whose selection is the creative act [20]. Newell and Simon's physical symbol systems hypothesis asserted that sufficient symbol manipulation is constitutive of intelligence [21]; the two standing objections are Dreyfus' claim that expertise rests on embodied know-how that rule-statement cannot capture [22] and Searle's argument that syntax by itself never amounts to semantics [23]. The distinctively mathematical version of the anti-mechanist argument is the Gödelian one, from Lucas to Penrose: that human mathematicians see the truth of what a formal system cannot prove, and therefore cannot be simulated by one [24][25]. Polanyi's tacit knowledge names the difficulty in the form most useful for this study: the competence by which a reader judges a proof is largely unformulable, hence not directly transferable to a checker [26]. Read from 2026, this literature explains why the current five positions are less a new argument than a re-run of an old one, with the burden of proof shifted from *capability* to *authority*.

### 2.5 The sociological counterpart: credit, authority, and the reward system

That shift has its own prior literature. Merton's Matthew effect documents that recognition in science attaches to prestige-bearers and to their sites of work, not solely to the propositional content acknowledged [27] — the mechanism that makes a 2026 authorship dispute a normal-sociology event rather than an anomaly. Kuhn's division of the scientific enterprise into normal problem-solving and revolutionary re-framing provides the classical ancestor of any criterion that distinguishes advancing a frontier from moving a border [28], and the position spectrum reviewed in Section 5 is, in that sense, a dispute about whose paradigm is being spent.

### 2.6 The fast literature, 2021–2026: benchmarks and position papers

The recent literature splits by genre. Measurement work supplies the denominator: miniF2F created a cross-system formal benchmark of Olympiad-level statements so that different provers could be compared on identical content [29]; DeepMind's peer-reviewed account of AlphaProof and AlphaGeometry 2 records the 2024 IMO result in the scholarly rather than the promotional record [30]; the formal-neural line continued with a prover reporting 88.9% on miniF2F-test at large sampling budget [31]. Position literature supplies the argument: Tao's capability conjecture and verification-bottleneck warning [32], the Leiden Declaration's proposed norms [33], the total-resistance case [34], the information-security framing [35], and the knowledge-collapse scenario [36] are the texts the 2026 camps actually quote. Between them sit the announcements that outran both genres — the FrontierMath benchmark and its correction [37], the unit-distance disproof [1], the Navier–Stokes claim [2] — which are the material of Section 4 and the reason a synthesis is needed before the debate hardens into allegiance.

### 2.7 What the literature does not yet provide

Three gaps follow. First, the three streams do not speak: measurement literature reports capability, philosophical literature argues about understanding, sociological literature describes credit, and no account links the *certification regime* under which a result is graded to where controversy is likely to appear — the correlation this study's ordering is designed to expose. Second, the objects of the 2026 dispute are institutional announcements and counter-announcements that postdate the surveys, so the record has not yet been submitted as a whole to a single set of verification questions. Third, the two load-bearing terms of the controversy — "crisis" and "groundbreaking" — are used diagnostically by no party: no symptom-list is attached to the first, no criterion to the second. Accordingly, Section 3 fixes the analytic categories and states the rival explanations the evidence will have to discriminate among; Section 4 orders the record; Sections 6–9 supply the interpretations that the gaps require.

## 3. Analytic Categories, the Syllogism Exposed, and the Observations That Would Refute the Reading

A commentary that ends in "more research is needed" has explained nothing. This section therefore fixes the vocabulary used thereafter, exposes the syllogism on which the controversy actually rests, and — the step that divides an argument from an assertion — sets that reading against the rival explanations the evidence must choose among, naming in advance the observations that would decide between them and the observations that would overturn the reading itself.

### 3.1 Five categories, defined for use

*Certification regime* names who executes the check on a result and whether a third party can re-run it: **machine-graded** (a checker or an objective counter decides; re-runnable by anyone), **hybrid-graded** (machine output is read and scored by designated human experts), and **community-graded** (acceptance is a slow social process of reading, using and citing). *Capability* is what a system can bring off; *certifiedness* is the standing a result acquires under a regime; *admissibility* is whether a candidate answer matches the question a community actually asked — a distinct property, since the same construction may be admissible to one formalization of a problem and inadmissible to another, as Section 4 shows for the Millennium Prize case. *Signifier and signified* (Section 6) separate the correctness of a derivation from the significance of what it is about; *frontier and border* (Section 9) separate a new theorem at the edge of a field from a new language in which old objects become addressable. The last three are introduced here only as names; their work is done where they are cross-referenced.

### 3.2 The core argument, reconstructed

The position spectrum of Section 5 shares one syllogism, and its honest reconstruction is as follows. Major premise: for closed games — fully specified rules, no appeal outside the system, positions decidable in principle — search plus learned evaluation has surpassed the best human play, repeatably and measurably. Minor premise as circulated: mathematics is such a game. Conclusion: AI will surpass the best human mathematics, repeatably and measurably. Read this way, the argument is valid and its major premise is among the best-attested facts in the record. What fails is the minor premise, and it fails by equivocation: mathematics is such a game *over the formalized fragment*, which is a proper part of the discipline — the nesting *quantifiable* ⊂ *computable* ⊂ *formalizable* ⊂ *mathematics* of Section 8 — and outside that fragment there is no referee who decides every position, because there is no agreed rulebook for which positions are worth deciding. Two consequences follow. First, the conclusion is true of the fragment and is therefore not the alarming claim it is taken to be; the alarming claim would require that the fragment is the whole, which is precisely what no party argues and what the evidence in Section 4 never shows. Second, the syllogism is not merely wrong but *useful* to its users, since it converts an unanswerable question (does the machine understand?) into an answerable one (does the machine win?), and the answer keeps arriving in the affirmative. An analysis that stops at "the minor premise is false" is however incomplete: it must still say why the same inference keeps persuading, which is the task of the rival explanations below.

### 3.3 Five rival explanations of the observed pattern

The record to be ordered in Section 4 has a shape that needs explanation: scandal and dispute are not spread evenly across achievements but cluster by stratum. Five candidate explanations, drawn from the literature reviewed above, make different predictions.

**(H1) The capability gradient.** Disputes arise where problems are hard, and disappear as capability rises. Predicted: controversy tracks stated difficulty. Observed against it: the 2024 Olympiad result (elite difficulty) provoked none, while the 2026 matrix-multiplication improvement on a fifty-six-year-old record provoked none either, and the two loudest disputes concern a benchmark score and a Millennium-Prize formulation, neither of which was settled by a harder problem. H1 is the account the announcements themselves favor, and the record does not support it.

**(H2) The announcement incentive.** Disputes arise where a result is disclosed by its producer, on the producer's chosen subset, before independent grading. Predicted: scandal tracks disclosure design, not content. Observed for it: the benchmark episode (a higher self-reported figure on a "chosen" subset, and a builder's organization disclaiming the run) and the 2025 Olympiad episode (a score announced before the next working day, graded by three self-selected former contestants) both fit exactly, and the case where a laboratory accepted official grading is the case with no framing dispute at all. H2 is strong and is the orthodox account in the sociology of science [11].

**(H3) The certification regime.** Disputes arise where the check is community-graded, and are structurally impossible where it is machine-graded. Predicted: dispute location is a function of regime, independent of difficulty and of who announces. Observed for it: every scandal in the record sits at or above the stratum where humans must judge meaning, and the silent stratum — objective counters, re-runnable checks — produced no dispute even when it broke a half-century record. H3 explains what H2 leaves open: why framing disputes arise only for results whose significance is humanly graded, and why a corrected benchmark could be issued at all.

**(H4) Institutional latency.** Disputes are artifacts of slow institutions meeting fast production: prize rules, journal queues, and authorship norms were written for a throughput three orders of magnitude lower. Predicted: conflict migrates to the institutions of adjudication rather than to the mathematics. Observed for it: the top-rung episode turned on the wording of a prize announcement, on an admissibility branch inside a formal statement, and on who had authority to say "settled" in public — three questions none of which is a mathematical argument.

**(H5) Resource concentration.** Disputes reflect that only a few laboratories can afford the runs, so the community's independent-check capacity is structurally outmatched. Predicted: disputes concentrate where reproduction is expensive for outsiders. Stated as a real possibility, and marked undecided on the present record: the public record does not disclose the budgets and hardware well enough to discriminate H5, and no conclusion about it is drawn here.

The five are not rivals in equal measure. H1 is the account the parties offer; H2, H3 and H4 are complementary layers of a better one, and the reading adopted here holds them jointly — H3 fixes where controversy is possible, H2 fixes how it is triggered, H4 fixes what it becomes once triggered. Section 10 states the resulting position; the ordering is made good in Section 4.

### 3.4 What would overturn the reading

Four observations would break claims made here, and their absence is part of the evidence. (i) A machine-graded scandal: a result accepted on a re-runnable check that later proved wrong in its formalization, or a benchmark whose machine grader produced a false positive. Nothing of the kind appears in this record, and the claim made here — that the purely machine-checkable strata have so far produced no scandal — is overturned by a single instance. (ii) A community-graded convergence: a contested high-significance result that passed into the literature without dispute and at no checking cost, which would show that the meaning stratum is not inherently contentious. (iii) A certified transfer of significance: a verifier that established not that a derivation is well-formed but that a theorem is *about* what its authors claim — the limit case examined in Section 6. (iv) A documented case in which a system moved a border rather than a frontier by Section 9's criterion, which would weaken the demarcation conclusion of that section.

### 3.5 The scope of the claim

What is being argued is an ordering claim — about where disputes arise and under which regimes — not a measurement claim. No new benchmark is produced here, and the one statistic computed over the record (Section 11.5) is reported as descriptive, expressly not as confirmation; a contested figure is cited as its source states it, with that source's reliability carried in the entry that documents it. The framing itself is contestable: a reader who holds the formalized fragment to be the whole of mathematics, or that significance is nothing beyond checkability, will find Sections 6 and 9 question-begging. That dispute is philosophical, and the aim here is to make its exact location visible rather than to settle it. How the record was assembled and coded — the corpus rules and their exclusions, the ordered decision rule for a certification regime, the coding cards behind the five questions, and the design that would convert the ordering into a test — is set out in the appendix (§11).

## 4. The Evidential Base: Layered Verification of Representative Results, 2024–2026

### 4.1 Scope of the evidence and the five verification questions

The evidential base of this study is the public record of nine groups of results, 2024–2026. Two earlier cases frame the record without belonging to it: in 1997 a machine defeated the world chess champion, and in 2016 a machine mastered Go. Both were victories over *closed* games — fully written rulebooks, no appeal to outside references, positions decidable on the spot by a referee. Game-theoretic research of the preceding century had already characterized such games as search problems in disguise; what the 2016 result added was the demonstration that search could be made fast enough to defeat human pattern-recognition on its own terrain. Whether the trajectory from chess to Go to mathematics runs straight, or bends where the rules run out, is the question this section addresses — through verification procedures rather than narrative.

Each of the nine groups is submitted to five questions: (i) was the claim honestly framed; (ii) is it reproducible by others; (iii) was it certified by independent experts; (iv) were failures disclosed as freely as successes; and (v) did the system *find* an answer already present in the literature or *construct* one that had not existed. The answers sort the results not into a ranking of quality but into a staircase of certification regimes: a machine-graded stratum, a machine-checkable stratum, and, at the top, a stratum where a human community must adjudicate meaning. Sections 4.2–4.9 traverse the staircase in ascending order of evidential controversy; Section 4.10 states what the ordering shows.

### 4.2 IMO 2024: the cleanest case on record

At the 2024 International Mathematical Olympiad, Google DeepMind's AlphaProof and AlphaGeometry 2 solved four of six problems at silver-medal level, including the year's hardest problem, which only five human contestants solved. The scoring was performed by two academics, Timothy Gowers and Joseph Myers, and the machine solutions were additionally run through the Lean formal checker [38]. The case is analytically the cleanest in the record because all three certification conditions were satisfied at once: a public contest, independent judges, and machine-checkable output. It is also the record's clearest instance of the kernel principle traced back to AUTOMATH in Section 2.2: what makes the certification cheap to re-run is that the checking function can be delegated to a small core whose verdict any third party can demand again on its own machine [39]. The open question was whether this certification standard, rather than the result itself, would generalize to the remaining cases.

### 4.3 IMO 2025: two laboratory answers to one verification question

At IMO 2025, two systems — Google's "Deep Think" generation of Gemini and a model from OpenAI — both reported gold-standard scores of 35 points out of 42, five of six problems; these figures are cited as reported, since the episode's particulars remain partly secondary [40]. The analytically significant fact is that the two laboratories answered the same verification question differently. DeepMind submitted its solutions to the IMO's official coordinators for grading under the same rules applied to students; OpenAI had its work read by three former olympiad medalists of its own choosing and announced its score during the closing ceremony, before the next working day — a "gentlemen's agreement" that laboratories would defer to independent grading was invoked on one side and not observed on the other. On the same problem, one referee judged Google's proof readable while another found OpenAI's barely so; former olympians repeatedly noted that machine proofs, correct or not, were uniformly fluent, and treated the fluency itself as evidentially suspicious. The route also diverged: OpenAI deliberately bypassed Lean, trading machine-checkability for the expected portability of natural-language proofs [40]. The episode thus supplies a natural experiment in certification regimes under otherwise comparable claims.

### 4.4 How a number is framed: o3 on FrontierMath

The OpenAI model announced in December 2024 illustrates the same problem at the level of reporting rather than certification. o3 scored 25.2% on FrontierMath, a private benchmark of several hundred research-level problems constructed by more than sixty mathematicians [37]. OpenAI then reported a second, higher figure — 75.7%, obtained on a self-selected subset of the problems — which became the more widely quoted headline; Epoch AI stated publicly that it had not run OpenAI's evaluation, and the trade-press coverage crystallized the dispute under the headline "manipulative and disgraceful" [41]. More consequential than the quarrel is its buried sequel: when Epoch issued a corrected benchmark (v2) in June 2026, the revision fixed errors in 42% of the original problems [37]. The episode yields two findings of general application: framing is part of a reported result, and a benchmark's own defects become visible only through public, self-correcting institutions.

### 4.5 The unit-distance disproof: the profession's machinery working as designed

On May 20, 2026, OpenAI announced that one of its models had disproved Erdős's unit-distance conjecture, a landmark open problem posed in 1946 [1]. Nine mathematicians, among them the Fields medalist Timothy Gowers, then authored a verification paper that converted the machine's construction into a human-teachable proof [42]; Will Sawin, in a separate paper, sharpened the exponent to δ = 0.014 [43]. The bounds now read n^1.014 ≤ u(n) ≤ n^4/3 — the lower bound newly established, the upper bound the classical result of Spencer, Szemerédi, and Trotter [44] — so the interval separating the two is wider, not narrower, than at any previous moment; the disproof settled the conjecture without shrinking the problem. For this study the case matters less as a result than as a certification event: proposal by machine, judgment by named specialists, refinement by the community — the profession's institutional machinery processed the claim exactly as designed. The result and its proof narrative are separately recorded in this repository [45].

### 4.6 First Proof: the bottleneck is checking, not solving

The Harvard "First Proof" experiment contributes the section's least comfortable datum. Ten previously unpublished research lemmas, prepared by Lauren Williams and co-authors, were offered as a challenge; participating systems solved at least six of the ten [46]. The reported difficulty lay downstream: human reviewers struggled to verify the machine-produced proofs afterward. The bottleneck is thus located not in solving but in checking — the profession's oldest and least-remunerated essential act [46]. The specific figures remain flagged pending primary records; a second batch of lemmas has been registered behind a nonprofit foundation [46].

### 4.7 AlphaEvolve: the silent stratum

Google DeepMind's AlphaEvolve found a 4×4 complex-matrix multiplication algorithm using 48 scalar multiplications, improving on Strassen's 1969 count of 49, which had stood for fifty-six years; across some fifty open problems in analysis, geometry, combinatorics, and number theory, it re-derived roughly 70% of the known optima by new routes and genuinely advanced about one-fifth of the remainder, including an improved upper bound for the kissing number in dimension 11 [47]. The figures are flagged as reported, pending a fully verified primary record [47]. What matters analytically is the absence of controversy: because the criterion — a multiplication count — is machine-decidable, no framing is available, and no scandal has been recorded at this stratum. The controversies of Sections 4.3–4.4 all arise one level up, where a human community must judge meaning.

### 4.8 FrontierMath Erdős: a benchmark graded by a machine

The "FrontierMath Erdős" protocol, announced in September 2026, closes the certification loop in a form without prior precedent: sixty-eight still-open Erdős problems are rendered as statements in the Lean proof assistant, and each participating model receives the same $300 budget to prove or disprove each [48]. The best score was 3% (GPT-6 Astra); all other models scored zero [48]. Because the grader is a machine, the two discretionary operations that generated the disputes of Sections 4.3–4.4 — subset selection and framing — are removed from the participating laboratories.

### 4.9 Navier–Stokes: the top rung, where meaning is adjudicated

In September 2026 OpenAI announced that an internal system had produced a finite-time singularity construction for the three-dimensional Navier–Stokes equations — a Millennium Prize problem — accompanied by a manuscript and a Lean formalization, generated by roughly ten thousand cooperating agents over several days with some 130 billion output tokens, the construction resting on a smooth external *forcing* term [2]. Three propositions, each separately evidenced, must be kept apart. First, within its own formal statement the argument may be valid: reports indicate the proof passed Lean checking [2]. Second, the statement falls within an admissible branch of the Clay formulation, so the prize's own rules may already treat it as a settlement [49][50]. Third, the mathematical community continues to regard the *unforced* blow-up problem — the question the field actually poses — as open, and *Scientific American* has reported a new argument that the OpenAI method does not extend to it [51]. The Clay Institute's announcement states only that the problem is "apparently" settled [49], while its standing evaluation machinery remains in force: publication in a qualifying venue, two years' residence in the literature, and general acceptance by the community [50]; the official problem page still lists the problem as active. A parallel dispute over authorship — a working researcher's account of an offer to publish a related result under his name alone, and the laboratory's flat denial — is recorded in Section 5.6 and flagged as contested [3]. The episode's analyzable content is therefore not the truth of a proof but the attribution of a sentence, the priority of a contribution, and the authority to declare both: the top rung of the staircase is not a mathematical argument.

### 4.10 What the ordering shows

Read without ornament, the staircase supports three conclusions. First, certification cost, not capability, discriminates the cases: AlphaProof's win shows that its work could be certified cheaply [38], not that its system is superior to o3; the unit-distance disproof shows that nine careful readers could convert a construction into an argument [42], not that a machine understood anything. Second, what degraded over 2024–2026 was not machine competence but the industry's claim-making — the boundary between a score and a fact, an announcement and a proof. Third, at the top rung the claim-making failure ceased to be a laboratory matter and became an institutional one. Mathematical content shows no crisis symptom on this record; the reporting and certification apparatus does.

## 5. The Position Spectrum: Five Published Positions and Their Shared Logical Skeleton

### 5.1 The shape of the controversy

Where one community might be expected to stand, five positions stand in the published record, and their disagreement is not about machine capability. It concerns what mathematics as a form of labor is worth: the dignity of the calling, the weight of a working day, whether a result obtained by a machine still counts as the same result. This section reconstructs each position from its strongest public statement, then shows that all five share one syllogism — the skeleton already reconstructed in Section 3.2, and the premise whose consequences Sections 6 and 9 now have to work through.

### 5.2 The integrationists

Terence Tao's ICM 2026 lecture essay, "Mathematics in the age of AI," argues from an explicit working hypothesis — a strong reading of the "AI Capability Conjecture": that AI tools will soon carry out a reasonable share of research-level mathematics at reasonable quality and cost. Should the hypothesis hold, the binding constraint shifts from talent to *verification*: proofs arriving faster than they can be checked, checked proofs faster than they can be read, readable proofs faster than referees can be found [32]. The same confidence takes an engineering form in Kevin Buzzard's long-running campaign to formalize Fermat's Last Theorem in Lean, with the stated aim of making "checked by machine" an ordinary sentence in working mathematics.

### 5.3 The institutionalists

The institutionalists answer with rules rather than visions. The Leiden Declaration (2026-06-02), drafted by sixteen researchers from fifteen universities, endorsed by the International Mathematical Union, signed by 4,181 individual mathematicians as of 2026-09-23, and amplified by a *Nature* editorial, enumerates five threats: plausible-but-unreliable machine arguments; authorship dissolved into black boxes; proprietary tooling splitting the profession into haves and have-nots; public-relations inflation of raw results; and the progressive thinning of the meaning of "understanding" [33]. Peter Scholze, among its featured endorsers, states the operative principle: the goal of mathematical research is *human* understanding, and mathematics can thrive only within a community of human mathematicians [33]. The declaration's omissions matter as much as its text: it requests no ban — only disclosure, standards, and authorship — a refusal to let external hype decide what counts as true.

### 5.4 The total resisters

Max Weinreich's essay "The crisis of AI-generated mathematics" argues for total opposition on the ground that AI is an anti-intellectual technology: it short-circuits understanding, devalues knowledge, and will tempt the best mathematicians to abstain from it merely to prove their standing to one another [34]. Patrick Massot has been reported describing mathematics as being "bombed" by AI. Within the fierceness sits one quiet, separable fact: verifying a flood of machine proofs is thankless and unglamorous, while the prestige economy pays for *discovery*, not for *checking* — so the current incentive structure punishes precisely the labor that keeps mathematics honest. The resisters' diagnosis of the incentive is sound; their premise that the incentive cannot change remains an open question.

### 5.5 The fatalists

The fatalist position runs the trajectory's logic to its end. Jacob Tsimerman is reported to hold the dark version as a creed: AI will destroy the field, perhaps the world, and mathematicians will have no choice but to use it regardless [52]. The position is internally coherent: if tool capability improves without limit and refusal is professional suicide, then each step of progress is a step toward ruin, and the only remaining variable is the manner of the field's descent. Its evidential status — a reported oral stance, not a published program — is flagged accordingly [52].

### 5.6 The moderates

The moderates, least quoted and methodologically the most cautious, draw the distinction later sections test. Bruce Schneier and Kasra Rafi write that current systems are strong at searching and recombining existing ideas, weak at constructing deep and durable new theory, and nowhere near a practiced researcher — a judgment published as commentary and flagged as such [35]. Michael Harris reminds the profession that mathematics has been, in his phrase, one of the last unalienated labors — a free creative art — and asks what of that dignity survives automation [36].

### 5.7 A case outside the camps

One episode belongs to none of the five positions and complicates all of them. Tristan Buckmaster, the mathematician who with Levent Alpöge proved the forced Euler blow-up in August 2026, reports that OpenAI, proposing a joint announcement, offered to publish the forced Navier–Stokes result under his name alone — setting aside his co-author for competitive reasons — and answered his threat to go public with something close to a career warning; OpenAI denies the account flatly [3]. This study takes no adjudication of the merits. Its analytic interest is the type: a working researcher whose results moved at corporate speed and whose authorship became a bargaining chip — a datum produced by the controversy while standing outside it. Methodologically, two readings of the episode must be held simultaneously, a discipline rarely practiced: an accusation may be true even though it serves the accuser; a triumph may be real even though it serves the company. Motive and content are distinct kinds of evidence — the first indicates who gains, the second what happened.

### 5.8 The shared skeleton

Running behind all five positions is one syllogism, quoted in the literature because it compresses the whole dispute: *Go is a game; mathematics is also a game; therefore AI will solve mathematics* [36]. The entire load rests on the minor premise, and it is carried dishonestly: "mathematics is also a game" holds only of the formalized fragment — a limitation every party knows. Consequently, none of the five camps doubts the *results*: the disproof stands; the Lean corpus continues to grow. What they dispute is the value of the activity itself — and there is the diagnostic tell. A crisis *of mathematics* would show symptoms in content: theorems collapsing, truths decaying. Nothing of the kind appears in the record of Section 4. What is being renegotiated is employment, authority, prestige, the meaning of a working day, and the standing of the judge. The crisis, if it has one, is a crisis *of the mathematicians*. Tao names the structure directly: the present moment is a stress-test of a largely implicit framework of mathematical values and practices — social, not foundational [32]. The configuration resembles the foundations crisis of the last century, with one inversion: what must now be made explicit is not the axioms but the working arrangements.

## 6. Theoretical Framework: Signifier and Signified, and the Limits of Formal Exactness

### 6.1 Naming the confusion

Every crisis narrative reviewed above turns, at root, on one confusion nameable in three words: signifier and signified. Mathematics is a language — a system of signs — in which we draw, exactly, what we want to say; and a language is never the thing it speaks of. The map is not the territory; the score is not the music. Much of the panic followed from quietly identifying the two: treating the formalization of a theorem's proof as contact with the truth the theorem states. It is not. The grammar can be flawless and the world unchanged. The clause "we draw, exactly, what we want to say" then carries a question this section must answer directly: *exactly* — relative to whom?

### 6.2 The arbitrary tie and the portable language

The confusion is built into the structure of every language because the tie between signifier and signified is arbitrary: "tree," Baum, arbre carry the same weight in their respective languages, and nothing in the wood demands any of these names [53]. The tie is arbitrary; the *structure* the signs evoke is not. "Group" is a tag for an arrangement of operations, and the arrangement does not care which tag is chosen. This is why mathematics survives repeated re-description: the language is portable, the necessity it carries is not. Mathematicians spend careers shuffling signifiers — number systems, definitions, categories — and what survives the shuffle, the signified, is the structure itself. Poincaré compressed the lesson: mathematics is "the art of giving the same name to different things" [54]. Names survive; necessity travels with the structure, not with the noun.

### 6.3 The disproof as a change of language

Read through this lens, the unit-distance disproof [1] acquires an eerie precision. The machine added no new fact about point sets in the plane; it substituted one signifier for a richer one. The same geometry, expressed for eighty years in Gaussian integers — whose symmetries are finite — was re-drawn in algebraic number fields, whose symmetries are inexhaustible. The signified never came on stage; the language of the drawing changed, and the answer became visible [42]. Control of re-description is control of mathematics' deepest operation, and the machine has demonstrated that it can re-describe.

### 6.4 The language works without certification

None of this makes mathematics arbitrary, nor makes it a first cause. The universe is not *made of* mathematics; mathematics is the most exact language yet found for relations that hold necessarily, whatever names are given them. Kant classed such truths as synthetic a priori: they extend knowledge yet hold independently of experience [53]. Formalism answered from the opposite shore: truth is consistency within a game of symbols. Gödel's incompleteness theorems (1931) raised a wall inside that game: no sufficiently strong consistent system can prove or refute all the truths it states, nor certify its own consistency. Turing's halting problem raised a second — precisely posed questions for which no algorithm exists — and Cohen showed the axiomatic ground itself can be rebuilt in mutually incompatible ways. The aftermath was undramatic: mathematics continued, and prospered, on what Tao calls "naive" foundations [32]. The foundations crisis that appeared fatal ended in resignation, unnoticed by the field. The oldest lesson stands: the language's usability has never depended on prior certification.

### 6.5 "Exactly" — for whom?

Consider now, without reflexes, who experiences a mathematical statement as exact. The profession's instinctive answer — "anyone who can read it" — concedes the point: the felt exactness belongs to whoever has mastered the dialect; it reports the reader's relation to the code, not the code's relation to the world. The difficulty is that this felt exactness is at once genuine and empty. Genuine, because within the calculus a claim is not trusted but *enforced*: a derivation either follows the rules or it does not, and there is no third possibility. Empty, because the enforcement never leaves the system. A proof assistant certifies the exactness of the drawing — every stroke matched against the grammar — and the grammar, not the object, is all it can see [39]. A formally certified proof may therefore be a proof of the wrong sentence. The Navier–Stokes construction with which Section 4 closed is, on the reports, exactly that: a proof, formally checked, apparently valid, while the community's actual question — the unforced blow-up — remains open [2]. The formalization was faithful; it could only ever be faithful to itself.

Beneath exactness lies a deeper layer: completeness. Within a chosen language, a theorem is as complete as its axioms permit; Gödel's wall is the theorem that no sufficiently strong language is complete about everything it can state. Quieter, and more consequential here, is a second incompleteness — the incompleteness of the *characterization*: every re-description captures some relations and discards others, so the signified of "unit distance" is not identical with the signified of "algebraic integer." The sense of exhaustion felt in 1946 — "we have said everything there is to say about the unit-distance problem" — was not exhaustion; it was exactness within the one register the field then commanded. The distinction governs everything downstream: exactness is a property of the sign-system; completeness is a property of the relation between the sign-system and what it points at — and no grammar has authority over that relation.

### 6.6 The limits of verification

Two corollaries follow, and together they discipline every claim reviewed in Section 4. First, verification — Lean checks, expert read-throughs — operates entirely on the signifier's shore: it certifies that the translation was faithful and the signs were shuffled lawfully; it cannot certify that the signified was worth attention. Lean's own reference states the bound in one sentence: determining whether a theorem has a valid proof and determining what the theorem *means* are distinct tasks, and the kernel discharges only the first [39]. Second, the freedom to change the language is at once mathematics' deepest liberty and its most human operation: no formal system decides which re-description deserves the next twenty years. That judgment — which language will let the truth show itself — is not theorem-shaped, and on the record of Section 4, not yet machine-shaped.

### 6.7 The classical objections, read from the far shore

The framework now in place lets us return to the oldest objections and give each its exact address. The Gödelian argument, from Lucas to Penrose, runs that a human mathematician can see the truth of a statement no formal system of hers can prove, and so outruns any machine [24][25]. Its premise is one these pages concede without difficulty: within any fixed language there are truths that language cannot settle — that was precisely Gödel's wall — and Section 6.4 noted that the discipline, on Tao's "naive" footing, declined to be paralyzed by it [32]. What the argument needs, and does not have, is the inference from "the human sees the unprovable" to "therefore the human is not a symbol-manipulator." For what the mathematician is credited with seeing is never the grammar, which is exactly what the system already possessed; it is the *adequacy of a re-description* — whether the unproducible sentence is the one worth attending to. That is the signified-side judgment of Section 6.6, the same operation that chooses which language deserves the next twenty years. The Gödelian objection, on this reading, locates no surplus of mechanism-transcending intuition; it rediscovers, under the name of insight, the very sovereignty of the meaning shore that this essay assigns to the community.

The neighboring objections converge on the same address. Searle's that syntax never yields semantics is the distinction stated from the philosophy of mind [23]; Dreyfus' embodied know-how and Polanyi's unformulable competence in judging a proof name, instead, where that judgment lives in a working mathematician — in the trained, largely unstatable sense of what a definition is reaching for [22][26]. Taken together the classical anti-mechanist arguments are not the essay's rivals but its corroborators: each, by a different road, fixes the human monopoly on the signified — significance, admissibility, the choice of what counts — and not on exactness, which was always the machine's to match. Section 4 states the same conclusion in a plainer idiom: through the whole noisy period the machines were exact, and no one had yet to ask them what anything meant.

## 7. Sociology of the Profession: The Ladder of Labor and the Deconstruction of the Genius Cult

### 7.1 "Mathematician" names a ladder, not a type of person

The first analytic move of this section is definitional: the word "mathematician" denotes an ordered set of roles — a ladder of labor — rather than a type of person. At the bottom stands the *solver*: the timed competition contestant, pattern-matching under the clock — the image that fronts popular accounts of the profession. Above, the *researcher*, proving things nobody has proved, most of them small, most by extension of a predecessor's step. Above that, the *theory-builder*, assembling results into an edifice — Weil, Grothendieck. At the top, the *field-founder*, who does not solve problems but announces that the conversation should change subject — Descartes, Newton, Gauss. Conflating the first rung with the fourth — routine in press coverage and hype cycles — is structurally the same error as concluding that the fastest runner would be the best architect. The confusion is not harmless: it spends the profession's scarcest resource, attention.

### 7.2 The top rung's working day, and the prestige economy under strain

What the top of the ladder does all day has changed twice within one generation: paper and ink; then verification at a keyboard; then — since the events of 2026 — deciding when to let a machine propose and when to demand a proof a human can carry. The prestige economy strains under the change. Working researchers in the Lean and refereeing communities report, in public statements, despair at an incoming volume of unverifiable text and warn of AI-generated nonsense polluting the commons of the journal system. The diagnosis added here is that the scarce good is not correctness but attention — and that the profession's attention-keepers (referees, editors, verifiers) hold the least-paid posts on the ladder.

### 7.3 Five measurable causes of the genius cult

Outside the profession, "mathematician" has become close to a synonym for "brilliant." The equation decomposes into five causes, none of them about aptitude. (i) *Historiography*: the cult of the solitary genius — Gauss, Erdős — is a storyteller's device that flattens decades of communal labor into a single flash. (ii) *Psychology*: intuition and rigor are trained as separate muscles and marketed as one gift; fluency in both is rarer than the stereotype admits. (iii) *Gatekeeping*: the olympiad system and the rituals of formal training select early and cheaply for speed and neatness, not depth. (iv) *Information asymmetry*: outsiders see only the finished proof — inevitable, clean — and never the years of dead ends that made the residue look effortless. (v) *Cognitive bias*: survivorship bias makes only winners visible; the halo effect lets one brilliant theorem license everything adjacent. The psychological components are measurable: meta-analyses spanning 177 studies and more than 900,000 participants find a real, reproducible drag of mathematics anxiety on mathematical performance [55]. The same public that reacted strongly to one benchmark's self-selected subset [41] has run the identical inflation on human beings for three centuries; the machine merely returns the compliment. The cult's mirror face does at least as much quiet damage: the crowd concludes, without evidence, that anyone who stumbles at mathematics lacks intelligence.

### 7.4 The job description

With the knot untied, the profession's actual job description appears, and it never read "be brilliant." It reads: carry the community's standards; extend what nothing else has extended; and at the top, at the cost of one's whole judgment and standing, decide what the community should work on next. These are categories of labor, not grades of intelligence. The sociological finding here is that these three duties are precisely what the profession has become unable to say aloud — the panic proceeds from identifying mathematics with the score (if machines score, what remains of the humans?), while the empirical residue — everything that judges, teaches, and cares — survives the machines' showing untouched, simply because none of it was ever scored.

## 8. The Division of Labor after Formalization: Capability Stratification, the Five-Stage Model, and Its Cross-Civilizational Correction

### 8.1 The stratification of capability

The relations underlying the section's argument can be stated as one nesting: *quantifiable* ⊂ *computable* ⊂ *formalizable* ⊂ *mathematics*. Logic occupies a special place — the skeleton of every formal system and, simultaneously, one of mathematics' own objects, studied mathematically by proof theory. The analytic weight lies in the nesting, not the labels: everything a machine has "solved" in the record of Section 4 lives inside the formalizable stratum — the Erdős disproof [1], which is why it could be certified, and every move ever played in chess or Go. The games trajectory and the mathematics trajectory therefore overlap on this stratum, and on no others.

### 8.2 What transfers across domains, and what does not

The transposable component is the *rule-governed core*: a domain whose correctness can be checked without appeal to context. Chess is fully decidable on its face, Go nearly, the formalizable slice of mathematics fully in principle — which is why one search technique keeps taking fortress after fortress. The non-transposable component is everything around the core: which game to study, which move is *important* rather than merely legal, which conjecture deserves the next decade. No referee can certify importance, because importance is not a position on a board. The trajectory runs straight precisely until the rules run out; then it bends — and bending is not breaking. The layer where it bends lies beyond the reach of any reward signal: nothing inside a search space marks which conjecture merits a human decade; reinforcement learning can grade a proof but cannot manufacture the asking.

### 8.3 Indifference to the obvious, and the asymmetry it creates

Within the formalizable stratum the machine's decisive advantage is no longer speed but indifference to the obvious: willingness to try a language a human expert has long since dismissed as irrelevant. Human mathematicians knew the right picture of the unit-distance problem; the picture was, famously, the cage. The machine, unbothered, tried another number system and walked out [1][42]. On bounded, checkable, searchable tasks, larger-than-human search now defeats individual human search without rhetorical qualification. The First Proof result adds the asymmetry's uncomfortable corollary: the machine can outrun not only the solver but the *verifier* — arguments arrive before human referees can judge them [46].

### 8.4 The division of labor, and the three emerging posts

From the stratification a division of labor follows directly, which is why the panic reviewed in Section 5 is aimed at the wrong target. Machines propose, construct, formalize, check. Humans choose the goal, choose the language, judge importance, hold the community's standards, and — in Tao's term — *digest*: convert verified results into something a mathematician can carry in her head [32]. Every bottleneck both camps predict — proofs outrunning verification, verification outrunning write-ups, write-ups outrunning referees — increases the scarcity of the human half rather than reducing its need: attention is finite, and the machine manufactures supply, not demand. On this model the profession is differentiating into three posts that the current decade is visibly creating: formalization architects, auditors of machine proofs, and interpreters who translate machine output back into the community's ordinary language. These three posts are not a new profession but the ladder of Section 7, differentiated: they cluster on the rungs Section 7.2 already marks least-paid, the attention-keepers who hold the community's standards, now called on as paid labour to absorb what the craft had long done as unpaid stewardship. The model's quietest warning is in that placement — the flood of machine output is scheduled to break first over the posts the ladder compensates least.

### 8.5 The five-stage model

Tao's ICM essay decomposes the single goal "solve a problem" into five sequential stages: solve, verify, explain, accept, and — slowest of all — absorb into the field's definitive theory, a stage he names canonicalization [32]. The chain converts the standing dispute into an auditable question: *which stages are machine-accessible?* Against the record of Section 4: the first is now machine-accessible within the formalizable stratum; the second is machine-accessible by construction — that is what a kernel is; the third, explanation, is the current frontier, since machine prose is produced routinely and fails to teach; the fourth and fifth, acceptance and canonicalization, are *constituted by* the human community and cannot be executed on its behalf [32]. On present evidence the machine has passed two and a half of five gates, and the decisive gates stand at the end of the chain, in the hands of the species, by definition rather than by limitation of ability.

### 8.6 The cross-civilizational correction

One correction, routinely skipped in the debate, must be entered before the section closes. The axiomatic, formal, set-theoretic tradition that currently calls itself "mathematics" is *one dialect* of saying precisely, not the language itself [56]. The Chinese algorithmic tradition — the *Nine Chapters*, celestial-source algebra (天元术), the Yang Hui / Jia Xian triangle — is mathematics of the first rank: it *computed*, by construction, what the axiomatic style proved afterward. The same pattern holds across the record: the Kerala school computed the power series for π and the sine roughly two centuries before the European calculus — whether the results traveled westward remains a scholarly dispute, and is the only flagged part of the claim [56]. Decimal place value, with zero operating as a number, is fully attested in Brahmagupta's *Brāhmasphuṭasiddhānta* of 628; the words "algebra" and "algorithm" descend, respectively, from the title of al-Khwārizmī's ninth-century *Kitāb al-jabr* and his Latinized name (Algoritmi); Babylonian sexagesimal place value, in use by the second millennium BCE, still governs the sixty-minute hour and the 360-degree circle; a positional zero — dot one, bar five, shell nought on vigesimal place — is carved at Chiapa de Corzo by 36 BCE, the oldest recorded zero written in place and independent of the Old World; and the Inka administered empire-scale bookkeeping on quipus, decimal positional knot-records, whose possible reach into narrative remains the open question dividing specialists [57]. Nearly every civilization examined here invented mathematics on its own ground, and the present dialect grew, by tribute and by conquest alike, out of the same polycentric tree [56]. When the 2026 system broke the unit-distance conjecture with levers — unit groups, algebraic number fields — inherited from that older arithmetic earth, it was reading every dialect at once, not one [57]. Wisdom has no nationality; on this evidence, the controversy does.

## 9. The Demarcation Question: What "Groundbreaking" Requires, and Whether Machines Have Met It

### 9.1 The criterion: border, not frontier

This section answers the essay's final question — can machines pose groundbreaking problems? — by first making the term precise. "Groundbreaking" cannot mean merely "hard," or the word collapses into "important theorem." A theorem advances the *frontier*; a breakthrough moves the *border* itself: a new definition, a new language, a field turning toward a new object. Descartes' coordinates made geometry algebra and algebra geometry; the calculus of Newton and Leibniz gave change itself a syntax; Gauss rebuilt number theory until "law" meant "pattern with a proof." The common thread is the re-description operation of Section 6, executed at the scale of a whole discipline. The criterion adopted here is therefore: *a contribution is groundbreaking to the degree that it changes the language in which the next generation can ask questions.*

### 9.2 Where the machine stands on the criterion

On the public record, systems have demonstrated the *cross-language* half of this operation, not the *language-building* half. The unit-distance disproof is a first-rate theorem — a paradoxical family of counterexamples, a long leap from number theory into geometry — but it resolves a question mathematicians had posed, in a dialect, algebraic number theory, that already existed [1][42]. The distinction of Schneier and Rafi survives the record: strong at recombination, unproven at theory-building [35]. No entry in the record shows a system constructing a genuinely new conceptual apparatus — new in the sense in which category theory was new, or sheaf theory. Read strictly, the games trajectory predicts exactly this profile: steady advance inside a given rule-system; no observed instance of founding one.

### 9.3 Wall-breaking: Zhang's theorem and the parity barrier

One refinement must be stated so that the honest counterexample keeps its full force. "Language-building" is not the only boundary-crossing that deserves the name breakthrough; the defeat of a *wall* that successive human generations could not pass is historic too. Zhang's 2013 theorem — infinitely many prime pairs within a bounded gap, first established below 7×10⁷ — was universally received as a breakthrough of the first order, and it was won entirely inside an existing language: the sieve and its near-century of apparatus [58]. The theorem invented no framework; it found headroom in the old framework that nobody had seen. The parity barrier of sieve theory then marks precisely what the old language cannot do: sieve methods are structurally blind to the parity of the number of prime factors, which is why they have never resolved the twin-prime gap of exactly two, nor Goldbach's "1+1," and why Chen's theorem of 1973 — every sufficiently large even number is the sum of a prime and of a number that is prime or a product of two primes — has stood since as the method's practical limit [59][60]. The lesson cuts both ways. Against the machines: "not yet language-building" is a real limit, not a rhetorical bias — the unit-distance disproof and the forced Navier–Stokes construction sit on the same shelf as Zhang's theorem, spectacular *wall-breaking* inside given frameworks. For the machines: wall-breaking of that caliber has been, across the twentieth and twenty-first centuries, the normal and sufficient condition for the profession's greatest fame [58]. If the standard convicts "who merely recombines the old language," almost every human giant is convicted alongside the machine; if the standard rewards "who moves a border," Zhang moved one. No advocate of Zhang's greatness, and no critic of the machine, has yet produced a formalism deciding which side of the barrier's edge either stands nearer.

### 9.4 The ladder confusion, repeated at the top of the ladder

The anti-AI argument now commits its own version of the confusion analyzed in Section 7.1: it sets the bar at "groundbreaking problems" as though that were the profession's ordinary output, when the measurable fact is that almost nobody poses one on demand. Hilbert's twenty-three problems redirected a century and now stand as a monument no one works in its entirety; even specialists commanding a single problem family are rarer than the polymath myth allows. Erdős spent a career *distributing* problems — the public database maintains 1,217 of them, of which 562 (46%) are marked solved as of 2026-09-23 [61] — and became the most famous mathematician of his day largely through that generosity. Most published mathematics is consolidation: extending, cleaning, sharpening a frame someone else built [61]. If "AI cannot pose groundbreaking problems" is the test, it prunes almost all humans too. Posing deep questions is rarer than the myth admits, distributed more unequally than the profession concedes, and not evidently a teachable formal skill — which places the slogan "tools can never pose deep questions" in the same category as the older "machines can never think": a bet dressed as a law.

### 9.5 The decisive case: Ramanujan's unposed questions

The position's strongest historical witness is the mathematician who never held a degree. Srinivasa Ramanujan — clerk in the Accounts Department of the Port Trust Office at Madras, salary £20 a year, scholarship lost, First Arts examination failed, college quit — wrote to G. H. Hardy in the winter of 1912–13; the letter ran to nine pages carrying "the enunciations of a hundred or more mathematical theorems," with no proofs, and closed: "Requesting to be excused for the trouble I give you" [62]. Hardy, expecting a crank, later described the first reading: "They defeated me completely. I had never seen anything in the least like them before," adding that the formulas "must be true because, if they were not true, no one would have had the imagination to invent them" [63]. The biographical frame — no degree through his student years, a Cambridge BA by Research only from 1916 — matters to the argument precisely because the questions were posed *without* the apparatus a trained mathematician would have used to state them [64]. Ramanujan asked what nobody else had asked: about partitions, about the τ-function, about mock theta functions. The mock theta functions of the deathbed letter of 12 January 1920 stayed unexplained for eighty-two years, until Zwegers's 2002 Utrecht thesis revealed them as the holomorphic parts of harmonic weak Maass forms of weight 1/2 [65]. The τ-conjecture of 1916 waited fifty-eight years for Deligne's 1974 proof, which had to travel through the full Weil conjectures to arrive; Deligne's 1978 Fields Medal was, in part, awarded for a question another man had dared to ask [66]. Berndt's count registers more than 3,200 results in the notebooks, almost all conjectured without proof, with about a half-dozen known to be wrong — and the false ones still pointed at structures nobody had yet seen [67]. As Gowers put it, it took extraordinary insight for Ramanujan merely to *ask* the τ-conjecture [68]. The analytic point: a man without the formal apparatus posed questions the language of the day could not cleanly state, and the profession spent most of a century translating them into its own terms; the verification lag was decades not because of sloppiness but because the questions ran ahead of the language that could answer them. If "groundbreaking" means posing what has never been posed, Ramanujan satisfies the criterion more completely than any machine on the record — and more completely than almost any named mathematician. The honest answer to Section 9's question is therefore: *machines have not yet posed one — and neither have most humans.*

The shape repeats a generation later under different skies. Hua Luogeng — shopkeeper's son from Jiangsu, middle school unfinished, self-taught between errands and a long illness, his first degree an honorary one — exposed in a 1930 note in the Shanghai journal *Science* that a celebrated 1926 "solution" of the quintic was fundamentally flawed; the note brought him to Xiong Qinglai, who recruited him to Tsinghua as a clerk and from whom the path led to Hardy's Cambridge [69]. Brilliance has never waited for the school; the school, at its best, comes looking.

### 9.6 The polymath interlude

The historical interlude is analytically load-bearing because it defeats the strongest form of disbelief in machine originality. Descartes, Newton, Leibniz, and Gauss were not narrow "pure mathematicians"; they were polymaths working philosophy, physics, mechanics, astronomy, geodesy, and metaphysics as one fabric, and their breakthroughs were cross-domain intuitions — the mystical secularized, the geometry algebraized, falling bodies timed. If what AI does is recombination, then so is every breakthrough the record contains worth the name: *cross-domain* recombination is the engine of the history those men wrote. The 2026 disproof is exactly a polymath's move — number theory imported into geometry — and the first such move the public has watched a machine make; its levers were a novel reassembly of long-existing deep tools, with the verification authors tracing the argument's lineage to the Golod–Shafarevich constructions [42]. "Creativity as recombination across domains" is thus not the insult it is sometimes taken to be; it is a faithful description of how Newton and Leibniz moved. The reservation that survives is correspondingly narrower and harder: not "machines can only recombine," but "machines have not yet demonstrated judgment about *which future is worth building*" — and, on the publicity record of Section 4, neither have many of the humans currently holding the microphone.

### 9.7 The deepest act remains social

The profession's deepest act — deciding that this, not that, matters — is a social judgment about value, exercised by a community over time. Machine output will flood the docket; on present evidence it does not decide the cases. What 2026 established is that border-*moving* problems are answerable by search once the language is given; what remains is choosing the language, and the eighty years of the unit-distance problem show that no referee, human or machine, can certify which choice was *important* until long after the fact. Importance is a verdict of history, not of proof. "Groundbreaking" is accordingly not a binary act of creation from nothing but a continuous spectrum — and at the celebrated, inhabited end of that spectrum, the public record contains, as of the close of the evidence window surveyed here, no machine footprint.

## 10. Conclusions

### 10.1 Summary of findings

Five findings emerge from the three analytic layers. (1) *On verification* (Section 4): ordering 2024–2026 results by certification regime rather than by claimed ability shows that the machine-graded stratum produced no scandal, while every controversy arose precisely where a human community had to adjudicate meaning; the failures of the period are failures of claim-making, not of mathematics. (2) *On positions* (Section 5): the five published stances share one syllogism whose minor premise ("mathematics is also a game") is true only of the formalized fragment; since no camp disputes the results, the controversy is already, on its own premises, about labor and institution, not about content. (3) *On mechanism* (Section 6): exactness is a property of the sign-system, not of what it denotes; verification certifies grammar and, by Lean's own statement of its limits, cannot reach the object [39]; beneath exactness lies the incompleteness of every characterization — which is why a formally valid proof can be a proof of the wrong sentence [2]. (4) *On the profession* (Section 7): "mathematician" denotes a ladder of labor; the genius cult decomposes into five measurable causes [55]; the job description — carry standards, extend, decide — was never "be brilliant," and none of its three duties appears on any machine scorecard. (5) *On demarcation* (Section 9): with the criterion stated as border-moving rather than frontier-advancing, machines have demonstrably crossed languages but not yet built one — and the Ramanujan test shows how few humans, including the canonized, have met that standard either [62]–[68]. Lying under the five is the explanatory verdict of Section 3: what the record's distribution of dispute recommends is not the capability gradient the announcements favor but a composite of certification regime, disclosure design, and institutional latency — and the four observations that would overturn that verdict remain, at the close of this record, absent.

### 10.2 The correct naming of the crisis

The title's question can now be answered in the essay's own terms. A crisis *of mathematics* would display symptoms in its content — theorems collapsing, certification failing as such. Section 4 records the opposite: the profession's machinery, from Gowers-and-co-authors digestion to Clay's deliberately unhurried evaluation, functioned as designed even in the period's noisiest episode. What the evidence supports is a crisis *of the mathematicians*: of employment, authority, prestige, and the meaning of professional labor — Tao's stress-test of an implicit framework of values and practices [32]. The naming is not rhetorical hygiene; it determines the policy agenda. If the crisis were one of mathematical truth, the response would be prohibition or rejection. Because it is one of certification and recognition, the response is institution-building: symmetric disclosure of failures alongside scores; benchmark ownership separated from benchmark citation [37][41]; machine-checkable protocols extended to open-problem formats [48]; authorship and forcing-condition disclosure standards for AI-assisted results [3][49]; and remuneration and prestige for verification — the least-paid, most essential rung [46]. Precedents for all five already exist in the record assembled above.

### 10.3 Synthesis and further research

The essayistic vocabulary of the controversy — "solve," "understand," "author," "replace" — collapses, on analysis, into relations of co-production: the unit-distance result *was* the meeting of a machine construction and nine human verifiers [1][42], as a working week made of many such meetings; the fear of human redundancy rests on a zero-sum picture of roles and a fixed stock of dignity, neither of which the labor-ladder model of Section 7 supports. What the machine supplies is patterns; what it does not, and cannot be shown to, supply is the wanting of them to be true — the demand side of attention, judgment, and care that organizes the whole profession. Three lines of further research follow directly: the pending adjudications of the 2025–2026 episodes flagged 存疑 herein, once primary records stabilize; the institutional effectiveness of verification regimes (e.g., whether machine-graded open-problem benchmarks displace framing disputes); and the longitudinal sociology of authorship norms as corporate-speed results become routine. To these three the essay adds its own preliminary census, set out in the appendix (§11) — corpus rules, the decision rule by which each regime was assigned, the coding cards behind the five questions, and two tables separating what is settled on primary records from what still rests on an announcement — so that each of the claims above can be contested at the cell where it was decided rather than at the paragraph where it was stated. The oldest division of labor needs no new justification: the banks hold, and the water runs; mathematics remains, as on the most conservative reading of its own two-thousand-year record, for the human who meets a pattern, wants it true, and wants to understand why.

## 11. Appendix — A Preliminary Census of the Eleven Events: Corpus Rules, Coding Cards, the Regime Decision Rule, and Two Audit Tables

This appendix is placed after the conclusions on purpose. Nothing in Sections 1–10 depends on it; everything in it can be checked against them. Its function is to let a reader re-run the coding rather than to agree with it, and to record where the reading's design stops short of what its questions would ideally require.

### 11.1 What counts as the record: corpus rules and exclusions

The population window is 1 January 2024 to 23 September 2026, the date on which the last link in the reference list was checked. Two pre-window cases (the 1997 chess and 2016 Go results) are used in Section 4 only to set the frame and are not coded. An item entered the corpus if it met all three of the following tests. (i) Its claim concerns a mathematical result, a formalization, or a machine-graded mathematical score. (ii) At least one artifact is public: a peer-reviewed paper, a benchmark technical report, an institutional announcement, or a statement by a prize body. (iii) It is quoted or contested in at least one of the position texts reviewed in Section 2.6, or in the coverage of those texts.

Recorded exclusions, with reasons: school and undergraduate word-problem benchmarks, which carry no research-level claim and attracted no certification dispute; vendor claims with no checkable artifact of any kind; results known only from a preprint that neither an institution nor a position text refers to; and continuous library work such as mathlib merges, which is infrastructure rather than an announced result. The unit of analysis is the *certification event* — one public claim together with the procedure by which it was graded. Where a single event was graded twice by different procedures it is split into two rows, which is why eleven events are coded from nine groups of results.

The sampling risk is declared rather than argued away: items enter the corpus through publicity, and publicity is partly manufactured by dispute. Section 11.5 specifies the design that would repair this.

### 11.2 The certification regime: an ordered decision rule

The rule is run in order; the first clause that fires assigns the regime. **Machine-graded (MG)** — the verdict is produced by a mechanical procedure over a formal artifact or by an objectively computable counter, and no human score can alter it. **Hybrid-graded (HG)** — a machine-verifiable artifact exists *and* designated human judges assign the accepted outcome, which they can decline. **Community-graded (CG)** — acceptance depends on being read, used, cited, or declared admissible by an unfixed human community, prize bodies and editorial procedures included.

Three tie-breakers are fixed in advance, so that two coders cannot diverge here. (a) If a human judgment can change the outcome while the machine verdict stands, code CG. (b) If a formal artifact is supplied for the *statement* only, and the answer itself is prose, code the regime at the level at which the answer is judged. (c) If the producer of a claim also executes the check, code the regime as though no independent check existed, and record the fact in the certification column. Clause (a) is what places the Millennium case at the top of the staircase despite its Lean formalization [2][49][50]; clause (c) is what removes a producer-run evaluation from the certified column without touching the arithmetic.

### 11.3 The five verification questions as coding cards

Each cell takes one of four values: `+` (satisfied on the record), `−` (not satisfied on the record), `±` (partially satisfied or contested), `n.r.` (not recorded — an absence of evidence, which is not evidence of absence). Every cell is fixed by the citation named in its row, so a second coder who disagrees can point at the same source and identify the clause of the card that reads differently.

**Q1 Honest framing.** Does the announced claim match the scope of the artifact? `−` where the headline carries a scope the artifact does not: a self-selected subset reported as a general score, or a result obtained under an added hypothesis announced without it. **Q2 Reproducibility.** Can a third party re-run the verification with public artifacts? `+` where checker and artifact are both public; `±` where a re-run is possible in principle but has not been demonstrated; `−` where verification requires the producer's private assets. **Q3 Independent certification.** Was the certifier designated before the result was known and free of employment or financial stake in it? `−` where graders are self-selected by, or are, the claim's producer. **Q4 Symmetric disclosure.** Are zeros, failures and rejected items disclosed at the same prominence as successes? `+` also where an institution publicly corrects its own instrument. **Q5 Found or made.** Was the answer recoverable from the literature, or constructed and previously unwritten? Descriptive; not an evaluation.

Coding was executed by a single coder (the author) on public material only. No interviews, surveys or non-public artifacts were used, so testimony about private motives is excluded by construction, and no inter-coder statistic is claimed anywhere in this paper. The cards and tables below exist precisely so that the coding can be re-run and disputed item by item.

### 11.5 What the coding yields, and what it does not

Grouped by regime and by the presence of a recorded public dispute, the eleven events give 1 dispute among the 6 machine- or hybrid-graded events, and 3 among the 5 that are community-graded or, like the September attribution episode (row 11), adjudicated by the same social machinery though not themselves a mathematical result. On this 2×2 Fisher's exact test returns an odds ratio of about 0.13 with a two-sided p ≈ 0.24 (one-sided p ≈ 0.20). The association is therefore reported as *descriptive*, and it is not the confirmation of Section 3's hypothesis H3 — at this sample size nothing in the corpus could confirm anything, which is why H3 was argued by the alignment of mechanism and location rather than by counts.

The counting is defective in one specific, repairable way: the denominators are selected on publicity. The repair is a census rather than a selection — enumerate the population of machine-graded mathematical outputs in the window (arXiv listings for formalized mathematics, mathlib merge records, published leaderboard tables), enumerate public named challenges to results (correction notices, commentaries, formal complaints, referee statements placed on the public record), and cross-tabulate challenge rate against regime. That design would test H3 against H1 and H2 with the same material the essay has had to order qualitatively, and it is the first successor recommended here. It is written out as an executable protocol — three sampling frames with their exact queries, one pre-declared endpoint, a power table, and coder-reliability gates — at [`submission/census_protocol.md`](submission/census_protocol.md), so that the successor study can be audited as a design before it exists as a result.

Predictions are stated here so that the next record can refute them: **(P1)** no framing or subset dispute will arise at a stratum whose verdict is executed by a machine and re-runnable by a third party; **(P2)** framing disputes will continue to arise only where the producer discloses before independent grading; **(P3)** where a laboratory submits to grading by a body it did not select, the dispute rate in that episode will be zero. The IMO 2027 results and any FrontierMath v3 release are the test set; if P1 fails, Section 3.4(i) is refuted and with it the staircase's central claim.

### 11.6 Negative-case search: how "no machine-graded scandal" was looked for

The claim that the purely machine-checkable strata have produced no scandal is a negative existence claim and was searched for, not assumed. Three queries were run against the corpus and its citations: reports of an unsound or exploited proof checker kernel; reports of a benchmark whose machine grader returned a false positive on an unsolved item; retractions, withdrawals or referee reversals of formally verified results. The one near-miss is FrontierMath's v2 correction, where errors were fixed in 42% of the original problems [37]. It is decisive to see where that defect sits: it is a defect of the *statements* and answer keys, discovered and repaired by the benchmark's own builders, not a false verdict by a checker; under rule 11.2 it is an instance of Q4 satisfied, not of the kernel defeated. The absence reported in Section 3.4(i) therefore stands, with a stated search protocol attached and a duty to revise: any single documented case of a machine-graded false positive would overturn it.
